## Inclusion decisions

Selection required human patient observations in peer-reviewed primary research, a distinguishable clinical phenotype, and complementary evidence supporting or qualifying inclusion. These profiles separate eye coordination, conjugate gaze range, vertical alignment and gaze holding from the existing saccadic dysmetria, pursuit and ocular motor apraxia profiles. Skew deviation is a physical alignment abnormality that can coexist with a perceptual tilt of visual vertical; it is not the same deficit. Vertical gaze palsy alone does not establish an entire dorsal-midbrain syndrome, and an internuclear deficit alone is not treated as one-and-a-half syndrome.

This is a targeted content review, not a systematic review or independent specialist assessment. The new profiles concern acquired impairments and selected focal lesion observations. Mixed-cause referral series provide context rather than population incidence or diagnostic specificity. Samples are not presumed independent, and patient counts are not pooled. Choi's cohort contributes to both horizontal gaze palsy and pontine gaze-evoked nystagmus; Moon's registry contributes to vertical gaze palsy and skew deviation. Reusing these data does not create independent replications.

## Source identity and extraction scope

The 11 included PMIDs, titles, author lists, issue years and DOIs were checked with the existing PubMed/Crossref reference-checking code. No linked correction, retraction or expression of concern was returned for these selected works. Moon's issue year is 2021 despite online publication in 2019; Walter and Romano have 2021 issue years despite 2020 online records. Only these 11 selected works were merged into the reference cache, preserving all earlier values. The cache's `fetched` date describes incremental verification, not a complete new retrieval of older sources.

Three references use full-text scope. This denotes consultation of the main article beyond its abstract, with the extraction described below. Lesion masks, figure pixels, supplementary case-level files and original oculographic recordings were not independently reanalyzed.

For Ewe, all three main case descriptions, the clinical summary table, imaging descriptions, differential diagnoses, follow-up and principal anatomical Discussion were read. Patients were aged 29, 38 and 46. Their lesions were considered inflammatory, with incomplete evidence for a specific established disorder; one had initially been labelled as stroke elsewhere. Vertical findings were not uniformly absent: upbeat nystagmus and mild upward-saccade slowing occurred. The paper's proposed revised arrangement of horizontal and vertical pontine pathways remains an interpretation, not a demonstrated tract reconstruction. Clinical improvement after treatment in uncontrolled cases is not presented as efficacy evidence.

For Choi, participant selection, bedside testing and video-oculography procedures, imaging and lesion-overlay methods, Results, representative cases, figure captions and Discussion/limitations were read. The 17 adults were aged 37–80 and came from six referral centres. Fourteen had MRI, three CT and seven video-oculography; the clinical cohort includes 13 ischemic strokes, one hemorrhagic stroke, two cavernous malformations and one multiple sclerosis case. One patient also had bilateral cerebellar infarcts. Left lesions were flipped for group overlays; this does not establish right-sided dominance. The paper reports overlays for 14 patients with gaze-evoked nystagmus and excludes CT-only hemorrhage/cavernous cases from MRI-based analysis. These reported counts are kept distinct from the seven with video-oculography; no supplementary mask membership is reconstructed. Sparse quantitative recording, difficult calibration in gaze palsy, nearby nuclear/fibre structures and possible enhancement of spontaneous nystagmus limit precise localization and mechanism claims. The pontine landmarks indicate the broad directly observed location; proposed vestibular, prepositus or paramedian-tract connections are not painted as independently demonstrated lesions.

For Lee, patient selection, eye-movement definitions, video-oculography, imaging and subtraction methods, Results, figure captions and Discussion/limitations were read. The 47 patients were selected from 229 cerebellar infarcts. Additional acute brainstem injury was excluded, with a middle cerebellar peduncle exception. Left lesions were flipped to the right; the overlap/subtraction analysis did not distinguish the unilateral- from bilateral-direction patterns. Common lower/midline lesion descriptions therefore remain observations, not a corrected voxelwise demonstration of a uniquely sufficient site. Vestibular imbalance can resemble unilateral gaze-evoked nystagmus, and structural sparing does not prove functional sparing. The article's animal/floccular background is not used to paint human floccular damage that was not directly demonstrated.

The remaining eight references retain abstract scope. Anatomical, age or task details absent from those abstracts are not invented. The catalogue now contains 64 full-text and 158 abstract-based reference records. Earlier addition reviews and the [full-text review log](full-text-review.md) retain their historical scopes.
