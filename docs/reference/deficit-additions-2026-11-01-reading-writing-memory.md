These profiles coloured no atlas region, so they were taken out of the catalogue with the references no remaining finding cites. ## Inclusion decisions

Selection required peer-reviewed primary human patient evidence and complementary evidence supporting or qualifying a distinguishable impairment. The profiles distinguish visually mediated naming from visual recognition; spatial reading errors from phonological decoding and semantic substitutions; handwritten character formation from learned spelling; and loss of pre-injury information from impaired new learning. These are clinical patterns rather than mutually exclusive diagnoses. Patients may have several impairments.

This is a targeted content review, not a systematic review or independent specialist assessment. Small case reports demonstrate possible dissociations, not prevalence. Publication samples are not presumed independent and their counts are not pooled. None of the five findings receives an atlas parcel: inclusion of a supported impairment does not require an unsupported anatomical localization. The existing hemisphere labels describe the documented samples specified in the mapping notes, rather than universal side requirements.

## Source identity and extraction scope

All 11 selected PMIDs, titles, author lists, issue years and DOIs were checked with the existing PubMed/Crossref reference-checking code. No linked correction, retraction or expression of concern was returned for these works. Absence of a linked notice is not independent validation of the findings. Cloutman's issue year is 2010 despite the 2009 DOI suffix and 2009/2010 volume designation. Only these 11 records were added to the cache; its date describes incremental verification, not fresh retrieval of every old source.

Four references have full-text extraction scope. This means consultation of the main clinical or experimental methods, results and relevant discussion beyond the abstract. Original lesion images, masks, clinical recordings and participant-level data were not independently reanalyzed.

For Kwon, the case description, visual assessment tasks and Discussion were examined. Tactile naming was 19/20, verbal semantic naming 35/60 and visual naming 0/60. Pantomime failed and semantic categorization was imperfect; memory, visuospatial and executive deficits coexisted. The authors describe a predominantly optic-aphasia presentation with mixed associative-agnosia features. Their right-hemisphere explanation remains a hypothesis.

For Galletta, participant selection, task procedures/scoring, models, Results and limitations were examined. Words/phrases were large, centred computer stimuli; articles/menus were paper stimuli with different layout, position and smaller print. Cognitive load also differed. The composite neglect measure had limited construct validation. The functional-reading disadvantage therefore does not isolate a single cause, establish general-stroke prevalence or demonstrate treatment efficacy.

For Riley, participant classification, category-verification procedures, accuracy and latency analyses and Discussion were examined. All eleven patients had aphasia and pseudoword difficulty; the five deep cases additionally made semantic oral-reading errors. Controls were significantly younger. Typicality comparisons were within each group, so differing significance levels alone do not establish a between-group interaction. The catalogue does not adopt a definitive semantic-storage or inhibition mechanism. An internally inconsistent control-education mean in the source is not reproduced.

For Billeri, both clinical descriptions, imaging descriptions/captions and Discussion were examined. Case 1 had left insular subcortical white-matter infarction; case 2 had left posterior internal-capsule hemorrhage and additional hand symptoms/mild dysarthria. Both case accounts and the Discussion report impaired copying, despite the Introduction's generic description of preserved copying. The reported keyboard/texting preservation is not supplemented with invented quantitative scores. The source's description of a pure syndrome does not exclude every subtle language or motor contribution; its uncontrolled short-term improvement is not used as treatment evidence.

The remaining seven sources retain abstract scope. Bayley and Cloutman's PMC XML responses contained metadata without main-text bodies, and their consulted PMC web pages did not expose the main text; availability of a PMCID is not treated as full-text review. The other five abstracts support the specific patient/task observations described above. No unconsulted lesion masks, statistics or detailed exclusions are inferred from them. Sakurai's Japanese-script assessment is not equated with alphabetic handwriting. Beaton's bilingual cases do not establish an effect of orthographic depth. Steinvorth and Bayley are retained together to expose differing remote-memory results, without treating their patient sets as interchangeable.

The catalogue now contains 75 full-text and 181 abstract-based reference extraction records. Earlier reviews and the [full-text review log](full-text-review.md) retain their historical scopes.
