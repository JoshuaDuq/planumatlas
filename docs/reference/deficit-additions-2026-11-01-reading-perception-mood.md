## Inclusion decisions

Selection required human patient evidence in peer-reviewed primary research, a clinically distinct finding, and complementary studies supporting or qualifying inclusion. The new reading profile concerns phonological decoding, the auditory profile concerns temporal order, and the visual profile concerns binocular disparity. These are distinguished from the existing pure alexia, interval timing and visual-recognition profiles. The two mood profiles describe selected lesion-associated symptoms; symptom scores and clinical case reports do not by themselves establish an individual diagnosis or a sufficient lesion pattern. This is a targeted content review, not a systematic review or independent specialist assessment.

Publications are not assumed to contain independent samples, and patient counts are not summed across published-case analyses. Earlier clinical cases may recur in later literature pools; the explicitly independent chart-review cohort in Cotovio is distinguished from its published-case cohort.

| Phonological alexia | [Rapcsak et al. (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18625494/) describe linked written and spoken phonological deficits after perisylvian lesions. [Dickens et al. Overall-reading voxel localization missed the corrected threshold (P = 0.056), so that finding remains uncoloured. Significant disconnection endpoints are not presented as direct lesions. Oral pseudoword findings are not generalized to every reading task. |
| Impaired auditory temporal-order perception | [Stockert et al. [Fink et al. (2006)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16460793/) compare order procedures and speech-discrimination tasks; [Oron et al. (2015)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25727346/) study 30 aphasic stroke patients and find comprehension/alertness correlations. | No parcel is selected. Stockert's maximal general lesion overlap is planum temporale, while its statistically significant classification result concerns posterior superior temporal sulcus and a broader impairment defined by at least two auditory subtests. Neither result isolates temporal ordering alone. The study lacks a right-lesion comparison group; healthy tractography does not establish directly damaged cerebellar sites. Task-dependent correlations do not prove that timing causes all language impairment. |
| Impaired stereoscopic depth perception | [Murphy et al. (2016)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27269606/) examine five chronic posterior-lesion patients using several disparity and motion tasks. Three with prominent right posterior parietal involvement show marked stereo deficits. [Schaadt et al. (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24376065/) document impaired stereoacuity and binocular fusion at repeated baseline visits in 20 stroke/trauma patients. | No parcel is selected. The five cases have heterogeneous and sometimes bilateral injury, with possible temporal or subcortical contributions. One retained stereopsis despite some right intraparietal damage. Residual disparity sensitivity and preserved reportable depth from motion prevent equating the finding with loss of all depth perception. Broad posterior parietal overlap does not justify a specific whole gyrus or sulcus. The training study supplies additional clinical observations, not randomized efficacy evidence. |
| Post-stroke depressive symptoms | [Weaver et al. (2023 issue year)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34547548/) study 553 ischemic-stroke patients with an independent 459-patient validation cohort. [Ríos et al. (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40620229/) analyze two prospective cohorts totalling 377 participants. | Only right amygdala and pallidum receive coarse landmarks: those associations replicated in Weaver's external cohort, unlike the hippocampal result. Ríos found no significant direct lesion association; its weak pooled connectivity relationship failed cross-dataset replication. That finding remains uncoloured. Different scales, follow-up times and lesion samples limit a universal anatomical model. Symptom scales are not substituted for a diagnosis, and no treatment-selection claim is made. |
| Mania associated with focal brain lesions | [Starkstein et al. (1988)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3276815/) report 12 cases with diverse lesion causes and sides. [Cotovio et al. (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32831292/) analyze 41 published mania-associated lesions and reproduce a connectivity pattern in 15 independent chart-review cases. [Lee et al. (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30340100/) provide an earlier 23-case network analysis and additional bipolar cohorts. | Both clinical and network findings remain uncoloured. Broad lesion categories do not resolve specific parcels, and connected orbitofrontal, temporal and frontal polar regions are not treated as direct damage. Cotovio's published-lesion set includes 21 of Lee's 23 cases, so those literature analyses are not independent replications. Variable symptom timing, clinical characterization and network methods limit attribution. Selected cases do not estimate incidence, and isolated disinhibition does not establish mania. |

## Source identity and extraction scope

Each of the 12 included PMIDs, titles, author lists, issue years and DOIs was checked using the existing PubMed/Crossref reference-checking code. No linked correction, retraction or expression of concern was returned for these included works. Weaver's issue year is 2023 despite the 2021 online-first record. Only the 12 selected works were merged into the reference cache; earlier cached records retain their values. The cache's `fetched` date records incremental verification, not a complete new retrieval of old citations.

Three references use full-text scope. This denotes consultation of the main article beyond the abstract, with the specific extraction described here; supplemental data and imaging pixels were not independently reanalyzed.

For Dickens, population and task methods, lesion/disconnection analyses, corrected results, tables/captions and the relevant Discussion were read. The oral task uses short pronounceable pseudowords and matched regular words. The lexicality-by-motor-phonology interaction is modest (P = 0.049), and generalization to silent reading is explicitly unresolved. The corrected sublexical cluster (P = 0.030) is anatomically broader than precentral cortex. The overall-reading direct-lesion cluster is below the corrected significance threshold (P = 0.056), although separate sensory–motor integration and disconnection results are significant. These results are kept distinct in the catalogue.

For Stockert, the clinical sample, audiometry, temporal-order and micropattern procedures, Results, lesion-classification method, figure captions and Limitations were read. Six patients were classified as deficit-positive and six as deficit-negative using impairment on at least two subtests. Posterior STS classification is therefore not an ordering-only map; the general planum-temporale overlap is also not the deficit-specific statistical result. Healthy tractography supplies indirect connectivity information. No cerebellar parcel is painted and no universal left lateralization is claimed. The article reports analyses for 12 patients while its Methods also mention an audiometric exclusion; no unreported initial recruitment count is reconstructed.

For Murphy, the participant descriptions, clinical/anatomical tables, stereoscopic procedures and Results, imaging/analysis methods and relevant Discussion were read. The five cases are aged 58–77 years and have stroke, anoxic or aneurysm-related injury. Control numbers differ by task; the 86-person Birmingham screening reference group is not misreported as the experimental stereopsis control sample. All cases are very chronic, some have bilateral lesions, and some performance remains above chance. The motion tasks and repeated-session design do not remove uncertainty about the precise causal lesion. No exact lesion mask, statistical cluster or functional visual area was reconstructed.

The other nine references retain abstract scope. Selected indexed main-text passages were also consulted for Cotovio's case overlap and methodological comparison with Lee; this does not upgrade that article to full-text extraction. Source sample sizes absent from an abstract remain unspecified. The clinical catalogue now contains 58 full-text and 141 abstract-based records. The preceding [full-text review log](full-text-review.md) and addition reviews retain their historical scopes.

## Excluded and unresolved source records

[Association of Lesion Location and Depressive Symptoms Poststroke (PMID 33504189)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33504189/) is flagged as retracted and was excluded. The linked [retraction notice (PMID 34092123)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34092123/) was checked. Neither record was added to the catalogue or citation cache, and its results are not used to support the new depression findings.

An additional older [global-stereopsis report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3581507/) was read at abstract level and did not support a lateralized evoked-response mechanism. Its retrieved PubMed identity record did not supply a DOI, so it is linked here rather than imported into the catalogue's DOI-required reference structure. This is a citation-format limitation, not a judgement that the report is invalid. Its different experimental method reinforces caution about generalizing hemisphere dominance. The profile's inclusion rests on the two clinical studies listed above. An older [1983 stereopsis report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6824530/) had no retrieved abstract and was not used for content claims. These screened records are not counted among the 12 included references.
