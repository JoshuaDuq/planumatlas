## Inclusion decisions

Selection required human patient evidence in peer-reviewed primary research, a distinguishable impairment, and complementary observations supporting or qualifying inclusion. These profiles separate awareness of a visual-field deficit from the deficit itself and from neglect; language self-appraisal from language performance and online error detection; object orientation from object identity and mirror discrimination; and object–colour knowledge from colour perception or naming. The language-awareness profile deliberately includes inaccurate or unstable self-appraisal rather than treating every discrepancy as denial of impairment.

This is a targeted content review, not a systematic review or independent specialist assessment. Patient counts are not pooled, and publications are not presumed to contain independent samples. Martinaud's 34-patient cohort contributes to both orientation and mirror-discrimination profiles; this does not create two replications. Its independence from the earlier case report is not assumed. Celesia states that the 32-patient cohort had previously been reported for positive visual phenomena. Healthy normative samples are not counted as additional lesion cases.

## Source identity and extraction scope

The 12 included PMIDs, titles, author lists, issue years and DOIs were checked with the existing PubMed/Crossref reference-checking code. No linked erratum, retraction or expression of concern was returned for these selected works. Baier's issue year is 2015 despite its 2014 online record; Pagani's issue year is 2012 despite online publication in 2011. Facchetti is a 2026 published primary study, not an unpublished proposal. Only the 12 selected works were merged into the reference cache, preserving all earlier values. The cache's `fetched` date describes incremental verification, not a complete new retrieval of older citations.

Three references use full-text scope. This means consultation of the main article beyond its abstract, with the extraction areas below. Source image pixels, lesion masks, supplementary clinical files and original task recordings were not independently reanalyzed.

For Van der Stelt, participant selection, two-session interview and naming procedures, awareness grouping, MRI/lesion methods, Results and relevant Discussion/limitations were read. Participants were aged 40–80 and at least six months after left stroke; severe comprehension deficits were excluded. Self-ratings were compared with two non-overlapping 60-item picture-naming tests approximately eleven days apart. The combined 24-person group comprised fourteen unreliable raters, six reliable overestimators and four reliable underestimators. Reliability and accuracy cutoffs were study-defined, not universal diagnostic thresholds. The effective lesion-analysis sample was 43, after imaging and coverage exclusions. Lesion volume was controlled and cluster correction followed permutation-based continuous familywise-error control. The anterior pars orbitalis cluster had cluster-corrected P = .03. The mid-superior temporal cluster concerned the comparison group with preserved awareness, and the wider anterior–posterior gradient was unthresholded. Neither is treated as another damaged site causing poor awareness. Lack of group differences in online error detection does not establish equivalence of every self-monitoring process. Everyday word-finding ratings, isolated picture naming, premorbid self-perceptions and selected comprehension levels remain distinct limitations.

For Cocchini, the recruitment, clinical sample, language assessment, visual support and check questions, discrepancy-score definition, cutoff construction, Results, reliability analyses and Discussion/limitations were read. The initial 65 patients had vascular or traumatic injury; two also had bilateral lesions. Nine patient and three caregiver check-question exclusions left 53 analyses. Ten showed clear unawareness and three were borderline. The cutoff used variation between caregiver ratings; caregiver judgement is not an objective ground truth and mild deficits can be missed by the discrepancy threshold. Retest data were available for 41 patients. The older severity association and the newer Facchetti study's null relationship are retained as differing results, not silently combined into a rule. Facchetti's abstract reports pathological or borderline distorted awareness in 37.9%; this is not relabelled as 37.9% definite anosognosia, and its under-/overestimation refers to deficits rather than ability.

For Martinaud (2014), the case and imaging description, experimental procedure, Results, main table, figure captions and Discussion were read. The patient was 75 at onset; the four controls had a mean age of 74.5 and more education on average. At ten days only the traditional mirror condition was administered. Accuracy was 40/45 and not pathological, while reaction time was markedly prolonged. At five years, mirror accuracy was 35/45 with P = .05 against controls and shorter reaction time; she reported favouring speed. The other experimental conditions were tested at follow-up and were preserved. These findings are not rewritten as the same severe accuracy deficit demonstrated twice, or as a full baseline rotation-versus-mirror dissociation. The lesion involved extrastriate cortex and posterior intraparietal/angular territory. Other visuospatial difficulties and mild later cognitive decline remained limitations. Dressing difficulties improved while mirror-task impairment remained, preventing a simple causal attribution.

The other nine references retain abstract scope. The linked full report for Baier could not be retrieved during this pass, so its regional result remains described without colouring a lateralized map. An available abstract is not treated as a reconstruction of corrected clusters. The catalogue now contains 67 full-text and 167 abstract-based reference records. Earlier addition reviews and the [full-text review log](full-text-review.md) retain their historical scopes.
